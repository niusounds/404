## The Pool Has No Bottom

Andrei Volkov was a man of measured things. For twelve years he designed water treatment systems along the Volkhov and Vsevolozhsk districts — he worked in flow rates, turbidity measurements, sediment load calculations. He knew what clean water should look like, even before the test results came back. If the effluent clouded at hour six of the settling process, the problem was not in the chemical ratio but somewhere upstream someone had quietly begun dumping something into the tributary he didn't yet know about.

He is probably no stranger to that particular kind of knowing when a system you've spent decades trusting has been compromised from beneath.

October twenty-first last year — the first frost had just arrived, the kind that cracks telephone wires and gives your breath the texture of shredded cheese — Andrey was standing near his dacha's well at exactly two in the morning because the pump had made a noise he'd never heard before. A sound most people would describe as a gurgle if they were polite about it. He described it instead as something pushing upward through several layers of mud, the way you might imagine a hand pressing up from below glass and then retreating just before your eyes could process what the skin looked like.

He lived alone at that dacha for two weeks before anyone noticed he was behaving strangely because on October twenty-seventh he had begun to walk around in wet socks. Not socks damp with spilled water — his own, pulled directly out of the well without wringing. This is not a metaphor. One morning when his neighbor Olga Nikolaevna came over to borrow sugar and found him standing at the window shirtless she called an ambulance and then called the district physician because they didn't trust each other enough for just one phone call.

## The Drowned Man Knows His Names

What Andrey told Dr. Yegorova in that moment — and this is on record from a medical consult note preserved somewhere in the Vsevolozhsk clinic, if anyone there hasn't burned their files yet — was that something had appeared to him three nights running now, standing directly at the far end of what used to be his neighbor's flooded cellar before it collapsed. The figure was seven feet tall and translucent at the knees upward where light caught through the thin mud walls but opaque below at the ankles because whatever lived in that water had decided to keep something heavy beneath its surface.

He said its hair hung like riverweed. He said its mouth opened too wide, like a pike's when it inhales before swallowing prey whole.

And then his body stopped doing things he normally did without thinking — eating regularly, sleeping through one night in a row, maintaining the temperature inside his own skin. His fingers were blue for three weeks straight. The local pharmacy kept asking him if he had been swimming overnight. They were right to ask because the water treatment systems had flagged a new chemical signature upstream and Andrey knew exactly what it meant: someone was dumping industrial byproducts into Lake Ilyumenka, and whatever that was had come down into his well without going through any of the filters his own company normally built for itself.

## What Lives at the Bottom Knows Your Name Before You Do

The third encounter — this part is from police reports filed in two separate districts between November and April, because Andrey Volkov stopped showing up to work on October thirty-first and by December he was no longer answering his door — happened near Lake Ilyumenka itself, which sits exactly eleven kilometers northwest of the Vsevolozhsk waterworks where he spent fourteen years building systems that never once caught what was coming.

The story Andrey told his sister over three phone calls before she took him to a psychiatric facility in Leningrad contained one detail every investigator on record noted as bizarre: he said he could hear it singing underwater, a tune without words, and when he surfaced from whatever had happened at the lake shore that evening — details of which are redacted because they were never confirmed or denied by any medical professional involved — his skin smelled like riverweed had been woven into a sweater.

What the local folklorists in their spare time noted is remarkable: every village along Lake Ilyumenka's banks has at least one story about what they call *vodjanoj chelovek*, the water man, who does not drown people but keeps them somewhere beneath himself like an object stored carefully under a floorboard. The reason these stories survive so thoroughly into modern Russia — past five hundred years of Soviet land reorganization that destroyed more than half the oral traditions in existence — has been attributed to one thing: whoever this entity is, it appears most clearly during winter when ice forms on the lakes, precisely because nothing else can get its hands on you from beneath.

The water treatment system flagged nothing about Lake Ilyumenka for six months after Andrey Volkov vanished. His supervisor at work never mentioned his absence in the files except to note he had not returned to his desk when his wife filed a missing person report three months later, at which point they both quietly closed it and said there was probably a good explanation. They did not share what that explanation might be because everyone already lived near water with no bottom somewhere.

## Beyond This Particular Story

The nearest thing to an answer I've found in the literature on Russian water spirits — none of it peer-reviewed, all translated through two intermediate languages and back again from oral testimonies gathered primarily by local historians between 1972 and 1981 before everything collapsed later — points toward a theory that has no place in any modern municipal water policy meeting: that the *vodjany* is not entirely mythical because at some point in pre-Soviet history, somewhere around seven hundred years ago, someone standing on the shore of Lake Ilyumenka saw something moving beneath the ice and wrote it down differently from what happened next time.

What I can tell you about Andrey Volkov is that his apartment remained furnished for another three months after his disappearance — no one claimed his property through any standard administrative process because the paperwork simply sat at a desk in Vsevolozhsk city hall for six months before someone finally decided to look up why the files had stopped. His water samples collected every Tuesday by an intern named Polina Sidorova were logged into the database until November third, after which they stopped being logged but continued collecting on a schedule that no one formally cancelled — at least once every seven days the same man from the maintenance department climbed the twelve flights of stairs to his building's rooftop water tanks and checked the turbidity with the same measured care he'd been given for eleven years prior.

He told no one why because everyone knew something about the lakes here had begun behaving differently during the long winter after Andrey Volkov disappeared. The lakes themselves still behave that way somewhere every night between December twenty-second and February fourteenth precisely because this is when any man standing on shore can hear a tune underwater with no voice making it from anywhere above the surface, exactly as Andrey reported only three weeks before his disappearance — which is something you should know if you have ever walked past a lake at midnight in northern Russia during deep winter.
