Dr. Kira Vane discovered the error at 3 AM on a Wednesday in March, when she was alone in BMA Room 12 — that is to say, alone until silence became *present*.

The tablet had been misassigned during cataloguing: KAR-7:12, pulled from the Assyur-bela-kala period (9. century BCE), expected to be a routine agricultural ledger about grain distribution. A quick scan of the cuneiform confirmed it — until Kira noticed that column three didn't list barley or wheat, but words that didn't exist in any canonical lexicon.

She read aloud.

Silence arrived like something standing behind her shoulder and swallowing every sound from the room.

The hum of the fluorescent tubes vanished mid-hum. The refrigerator in her apartment building downstairs — she could have sworn it had cut out — held its breath. Even the blood rushing through her ears simply… stopped. It was not quiet. It was absence. Sound had been evicted by something that did not permit its existence within four walls.

Kira read on. Her voice had become hers to hear only; no vibration left her lips. She saw the word **lilim** again, repeated three times in the same column. It appeared twice more before she realized — *three times was always wrong for Sumerian enumeration*. They wrote 2+1 or 3+0 but never tripled a symbol on its own without grammatical sense. Unless the meaning was "the sum of uncounted things."

When the silence released her, it did so by pushing sound back in like a wave retreating through a door. She nearly fainted.

She took a photo of the column and walked to the museum's director for confirmation. She showed him nothing — not because she had lost nerve, but because *between leaving the reading room and crossing through the corridor*, every word on her face had fallen quiet. Not erased from thought but sealed, as if an ancient seal had been pressed onto the soft tissue of her jaw while she was not watching herself move.

She went home that night by taxi. The driver asked where to. She pointed and opened her mouth. Her mouth worked — she could see it work from the rearview mirror — but no sound came out. Only air, cold and weightless, pushed through like a page turning without ink.

The next day, the tablet was catalogued as **KAR-7:12: UNKNOWN** under field notes Kira submitted via email (she could still write). Then came three more tablets from the same excavation season — NIM-4:09, AHM-3:22, and UR-8B:06. Each contained repetitions of **lilim**, written in a slightly different hand each time but describing the *same* collection ritual.

According to these fragments, the Lilim were not demons of violence or possession. They were archivists of the Netherworld, serving beneath the gates of Irkalla. Their job: to wait at the border between breath and stillness, collecting voices from people who had just been silenced — those cut off mid-speech, suffocating victims, the last words before a drowning man went under. They didn't eat it or destroy it. They *filed* it.

Kira spent two weeks cross-referencing with the Enuma Anu Enlil tablets from Nineveh and the *Utukkû Lemnūtu* exorcism corpus. She discovered that in every Mesopotamian text describing an "unresolved death," the phrase "lilim will take the voice" appeared within two lines of the casualty count. Never outside those bounds. The Lilim had rules. Boundaries they could not cross unless someone on earth *named them aloud*, three times, during a moment where their own breath stopped — which is to say: in that exact instant of silence, before sound returned, when the boundary between living and already-dead was thinnest.

Kira realized something had to be true about her experience at 3 AM. She had *nearly* done what she now knew not to do: speak their names while a moment of stillness caught itself around her.

It did catch. For seven seconds exactly — the duration she measured by watching the wall clock on her drive home when her voice finally returned.

Seven seconds is, apparently, how long an archivist needs to file something precious.*
