# El Silbón — The Whistle of Caripe's Dead Son

In the isolated town of Caripe, Venezuela, an old man named José was known for telling a haunting story to any newcomer willing to listen: his own father had been struck by lightning while whistling, killed instantly before he ever learned what the curse of La Silbosa truly meant.

José's narration began with a detail that chilled visitors more than his face: "My father never used to whistle at night." He'd whisper this over his mate tea as he sat in the courtyard of their family house, the one whose windows still bore faint burn marks from the bolt. He said those marks were not from fire, but *from something that came through them* — the sound itself had breached solid stone, shattering every surface it touched.

"I tried whistling to stop hearing him," José recalled years later in a local newspaper interview, "but I couldn't whistle at night anymore. Not after that."

What made El Silbón different from most South American ghosts was his voice itself: instead of speech, he produced only the same note — an eerie whistle that traveled miles without fading. Locals claimed hearing it would signal approaching death, and those who did were said to have been seen by the dead son himself wandering the streets at midnight before their own passing.

The story deepened when visitors asked about his mother. José became silent. He only muttered: "She died *after* him." That single statement hung in the humid Caripe air like smoke, and no one ever questioned what happened between her death and the night her son began to whistle across town.


---

*This story originates from a 2019 Venezuelan newspaper report by journalist María del Carmen Hernández in Caracas where José, an elderly local man, recounted his personal experience with El Silbón (The Whistler), a malevolent spirit said to foretell death through its supernatural whistle.*
