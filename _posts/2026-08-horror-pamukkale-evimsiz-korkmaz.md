## Bone-Dissolving Shadows

The limestone cliffs of Cappadocia glow rose-gold at dawn. Every travel guide in the world writes about it - "1000 fairy chimneys, underground cities, a place where the earth remembers itself." They photograph the valleys in warm light and call it beautiful. The locals do not share their vocabulary for this particular moment before sunrise.

The thing they have no word for is what comes after darkness breaks.

In Turkish I call them *Evimsizler* - those with no homes, those without bodies. There is an older word from the villages beyond Goreme that translates more accurately as "those who need to borrow another's heat." The tourists on their balloon rides have heard neither term, because nobody talks about it when there are visitors watching.

But in 2017 a woman named Elif Alpata came to Cappadocia with an official assignment she describes only as administrative work for the district office. She had written three letters home from different hotels between March and October that year. The first letter contained nothing more than complaints about the hotel's heating system. The second was addressed "to whomever it may concern" - she never sent it, or if she did, no copy exists in any municipal archive. The third letter arrived at her sister's address with only two sentences:

> *They take your temperature and leave you cold.*
>
> *Do not come to the valleys after dark.*

This is what the local hospital emergency room staff would tell you if they are willing to speak at all: patients whose body temperatures drop without explanation, people who present in January wearing only summer clothes. The official diagnoses always point toward thyroid failure or environmental exposure. One doctor in Caykur, a man named Dr. Yilmaz Korkmaz, reportedly kept notes on this pattern for years and then stopped - his handwriting deteriorates sharply from August 2018 onward and he retired early that October.

## What the 19th-century records tell us

The oldest surviving accounts of *Evimsizler* come from an Orthodox monk named Efstathios who wrote to Constantinople in 1874 about villagers who "appear at dawn as though composed entirely of bone." They speak, they move, they carry water and do labor. But they leave no footprints, they cast no shadow, and their bones can be heard rattling inside themselves when the wind blows from an unusual direction.

A physician in Smyrna documented similar patients three decades later. He wrote: "Their tissues dissolve not through disease but through cold so absolute that what remains cannot be classified as any state of matter I recognize."

The villagers, when asked, described the phenomenon this way: the shadows at sunset gather. They are drawn away from the cliff faces where they sleep, and begin to drift toward warmth - specifically human warmth. A body's core temperature hovers around 37 degrees Celsius, which represents a concentration of heat that these hollow things mistake for residence.

Once one such entity settles inside a body it cannot sustain itself without constant consumption. The victim loses weight rapidly. Then they lose water. Finally only the calcium framework remains - and even this dissolves as the creature feeds on residual marrow warmth. Within weeks, nothing recognizable survives of what was once a person. What is left is often described by those who encounter it first as "a pile of warm dust arranged in the shape of clothing."

## The pattern that matches across centuries

Every account from 1803 onward shares one detail nobody mentioned to tourists or anyone willing to publish: the shadows come when temperatures drop without explanation. Not in winter - in summer, unexpectedly. The locals know these days differently than weather maps do. They watch their own body temperature with something approaching alarm, because at night they believe the *Evimsizler* are hunting for warmth.

What happens during those nights is always described the same way by witnesses who have lived long enough to forget fear: a sound like glass settling. A coldness that does not come from wind or rain but originates internally - as though something has decided your skeleton no longer belongs exclusively to you.

I found one last note inside Elif Alpata's belongings after her death, written on hotel stationery with ink barely legible. It reads:

> *Do not look away from the cliff edge at dusk. They are already gone by morning.*
>

Her body was never recovered from those valleys during summer months according to Turkish municipal records - a detail that does appear in any official search report filed between August and early October of that year. The district office closed her case on administrative grounds, which is the kind of explanation only someone who has heard the story before finding it useful.

The limestone cliffs still glow rose gold at dawn across Anatolia. Tourists photograph them. Locals close their windows before that last dark hour passes - because whatever comes out after night falls wants a place to stay inside you.</article>
